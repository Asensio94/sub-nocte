Sub Nocte

ibant obscuri sola sub nocte per umbram — Virgilio, Eneida VI

Every spring and autumn night, millions of birds cross Europe in the dark. A handful of nights carry half of the passage. This page tries to say which ones, city by city, so that the lights can go out on exactly those nights.

Technical preview, not a production service. The model is validated (see below), but none of these cities has a radar close enough to check the forecast the next morning. Treat it as an indication, not a settled figure.

What to do on an alert night

What actually reduces collisions and disorientation, ordered by effect and by ease.

Switch off decorative lighting

Façades, monuments, skybeams and non-essential signs, from sunset to dawn.

Switch off empty floors and offices

Lit upper floors of glass buildings are the ones that attract most birds and kill most of them.

Draw blinds and curtains

If the indoor lights have to stay on, keep the light from spilling out of the window.

Aim the light at the ground

Luminaires with all their output below the horizontal and a warm colour temperature (≤ 2,700 K); blue light is more disorienting.

How it is computed

No acronyms, in four steps.

  1. Weather radars see birds. Besides rain, a radar measures the echo of the animals crossing its beam. The European network openly publishes a vertical profile every 5-15 minutes giving how many birds there are per cubic kilometre at each height. We summarise each night in a single figure: the mean bird density in the air column, in birds per square kilometre.
  2. Weather explains a good part of that figure. Using eleven years of nights from 55 radars in Spain, Portugal and France we train two models: one estimates how many birds there will be and the other decides whether the night will carry heavy passage. The variables are the wind at the heights where they fly (750, 1,500 and 3,000 metres), split into the part pushing along the migratory heading and the part pushing sideways; temperature and its 24-hour change; humidity, cloud cover, rain and pressure; and the day of the year.
  3. Validation leaves whole radars out, not random nights. It is the only honest test for a city without a radar: the model is trained without that radar and then asked to predict it blind. Done that way it captures 34 % of the heavy-passage nights against the 10 % that chance would give, with 66 % false alarms. In other words: three times better than a coin flip, and wrong fairly often.
  4. The alert is calibrated city by city. The model is run over five years of weather at the city's exact location and the levels are cut at the percentiles of that distribution. That is why the alert needs no radar in the city and means the same thing everywhere.

What this is not

  • It is not a count of the birds over your roof: it is the mean density across the whole air column, most of it between 200 and 3,000 metres up.
  • It does not tell species apart. A weather radar cannot know whether the echo is a thrush or a warbler.
  • In autumn and in the south, part of the signal may be insects. Separating them needs flight speed, and the European archive stopped publishing it in France in 2021 and almost always in Spain.
  • The forecast decays with lead time: the first night is reliable, the seventh much less so.
  • The four Spanish cities with a renewed radar are the model's hard case (it ranks their nights reasonably well, but the profile arrives truncated to six layers). Firm decisions there are better left until the 2027 season.
