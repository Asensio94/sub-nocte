Sub Nocte

ibant obscuri sola sub nocte per umbram — Virgil, Aeneid VI

Every spring and autumn night, millions of birds cross Europe in the dark. A handful of nights carry half of the passage. This page tries to say which ones, city by city, so that the lights can go out on exactly those nights.

Wind farms

Updated on 30 October 2026. This table covers the 151 wind zones in Spain and Portugal (24,529 turbines); the wind farms page adds the French ones too, so its per-night figures are larger.

The same heavy-passage nights, seen from a wind farm. In the Netherlands the offshore farms are already stopped on nights of intense migration with a forecast like this one; in Spain and Portugal nothing like it is in place. For each zone (a half-degree cell, about 55 × 42 km) two things are crossed: how much passage is expected (the same model as the cities, calibrated on the 2021-2026 weather from ERA5, Copernicus's reconstruction of past weather, at the zone's point) and what stopping costs (the output the turbines would have that night with the wind forecast at 100 m).

Stopping is only suggested when the passage is very high and stopping is cheap, that is, on nights with little wind. Tested on 2021-2026 at the 151 zones in Spain and Portugal, that rule would have stopped 7.5 nights per season and zone, for 3.1% of the night output, and covered 8.8% of the predicted migration; stopping on every very high night would have cost 12.8%. And it picks the right nights: the nearest radar measured heavy passage on 67.5% of them, against 24.3% by chance.

What to do on an alert night

What actually reduces collisions and disorientation, ordered by effect and by ease.

Switch off decorative lighting

Façades, monuments, skybeams and non-essential signs, from sunset to dawn.

Switch off empty floors and offices

Lit upper floors of glass buildings are the ones that attract most birds and kill most of them.

Draw blinds and curtains

If the indoor lights have to stay on, keep the light from spilling out of the window.

Aim the light at the ground

Luminaires with all their output below the horizontal and a warm colour temperature (≤ 2,700 K); blue light is more disorienting.

How it is computed

From a radar echo to a lights-out alert, in nine steps and without acronyms.

  1. A weather radar sees birds too

    A weather radar is an antenna that turns and sends out microwave pulses. When a pulse hits something — raindrops, a bird, an insect — part of it comes back. The radar measures how much energy returns, which says how much “material” there is, and how long it took, which says how far away it is. To look at different heights it repeats the turn with the beam tilted more and more, from half a degree to over ten. Every 5-15 minutes it has a full volume of the air around it.

    It also measures the Doppler effect: the small change in frequency of the echo when what reflects it moves towards or away from the antenna, like an ambulance siren changing pitch as it passes. That is where the birds' speed will come from.

  2. The air, cut into 200-metre slices

    An open program, vol2bird (Dokter et al. 2011), takes each volume, keeps the ring between 5 and 35 km from the radar and cuts it into horizontal slices 200 m thick. For each slice it computes:

    • Density: the total echo divided by the echo of a typical bird, a small songbird with 11 cm² of “radar surface”. It comes out in birds per cubic kilometre.
    • Speed and direction: if the birds head north-east, on that side of the radar they move away and on the opposite side they come closer. Fitting that pattern all the way round gives where the whole lot is going and how fast.
    • Disorder: how far the measured speeds stray from that common movement. Rain and insects drift with the wind and give a clean pattern; birds each fly their own heading and give a messier one. A slice with less than 2 m/s of disorder counts as zero birds.

  3. From the weather service to this page

    The Spanish radars belong to AEMET, the Portuguese ones to IPMA and the French ones to Météo-France. Each service sends its volumes to OPERA, the network that pools the weather radars of almost all of Europe. Aloft, a European scientific archive, runs vol2bird on those volumes and publishes the profiles openly (CC0), one or two days late and without quality control. Sub Nocte downloads them from there: one file per radar and day, with one row per slice and per profile.

    How many radars. Each figure counts something different: 56 radars have some data in the 2016-2026 record (islands included); 55 have data since 2021 and go into the model; on any given night about 35-40 publish, the ones quoted under «The latest night on the radars»; and the routes map shows 43 stations, because a radar renewed on the same site as the old one counts as one and the Azores and the Canaries are off the map.

  4. One night, one figure

    The slices between 200 and 3,000 m are added up, each one as its density times 0.2 km of thickness. The result is how many birds there are in the air column over one square kilometre of ground. Then all the profiles of the night are averaged, from when the sun drops 6° below the horizon until it climbs back past it. That is the page's central figure: the nightly VID, in birds/km².

    VID = Σ density × 0.2 km  →  mean over the night

    BirdCast uses another unit: how many birds cross an imaginary 1 km line during the night (MTR). It is density times speed, so it needs the speed. Here it is computed only when at least half of the night's density has a measured speed; otherwise it is left empty, never set to zero.

  5. What a heavy night is, place by place

    From each radar's record since 2016 we work out, for every day of the year, what a normal night looks like and where the 70th and 90th percentiles fall (the figures only 30 % and 10 % of nights exceed). Sanity check: the most intense 10 % of nights carry 50-55 % of the season's passage in Spain, Portugal and France, the same share measured in the United States (54 %).

  6. Weather explains a good part of that figure

    Using the nights of 2021 to 2026, the years that come with wind at flight height, from the 55 radars in Spain, Portugal and France with data in those years (of the 56 in the record, one stopped publishing in 2020) we train two models: one estimates how many birds there will be and the other decides whether the night will carry heavy passage. The variables are the wind at the heights where they fly (750, 1,500 and 3,000 metres), split into the part pushing along the migratory heading and the part pushing sideways; temperature and its 24-hour change; humidity, cloud cover, rain and pressure; and the day of the year.

  7. Validation leaves whole radars out

    Not random nights. It is the only honest test for a city without a radar: the model is trained without that radar and then asked to predict it blind. Done that way it captures 34 % of the heavy-passage nights (here, the busiest 10 % of each radar) against the 10 % that chance would give, with 66 % false alarms. It is the 34 % at the top of the home page. Three times better than a coin flip, and wrong fairly often.

  8. The alert is calibrated city by city and date by date

    The model is run over the weather from 2021 to today at the city's exact location, and the levels are cut at the percentiles of the nights around the same date, in a window of three weeks either side: moderate above the median, high above the 75th percentile, very high above the 90th. That is why the alert needs no radar in the city, means the same thing everywhere and still means something at the peak of the passage.

  9. Every forecast is checked afterwards

    Each day's forecast stays in the repository history with its date. Every Monday the past ones are retrieved, what the radars measured afterwards is downloaded, and the result is published in the verification report.

Two gaps in the data

Going through the files slice by slice turns up two shortcomings that are not in the archive's documentation. They are measured, not assumed, and they explain a good part of why the Spanish cities are the hard case.

In Spain the radar only looks at the first kilometre

On AEMET's renewed radars there is echo only up to about 900-1,000 m above the antenna: five or six slices. Above that the file carries not a single measurement, of birds or of anything else. With the same program, Porto reaches 3.7 km above the antenna and Nantes 4.7 km.

The most likely explanation is geometric. At 35 km from the radar, a beam tilted 1.5° passes a little over 900 m above the antenna, plus about 100 m more because of the Earth's curvature. Only the steeper beams see the higher layers. If only the lowest turns reach the European network, the ceiling lands right there. It is a hypothesis still to be confirmed with AEMET.

Consequence: birds flying higher are not counted. That is why a Spanish radar's figure is never compared with another country's here: the levels are each radar's own percentiles.

The program that computes it is the same, so the most likely cause is a change in how the Doppler data reaches the European network. On the renewed Spanish radars it is there in a bit under half of the slices with birds in spring, and almost never in winter, when there are hardly any birds. AEMET's old radars always gave it.

Consequence: without speed there is no traffic rate (MTR) and no disorder filter to set insects apart. That is why the central figure is the VID, which needs only density. Still to ask Aloft whether the Doppler data is in the source files and whether 2021-2026 can be reprocessed.

What this is not

  • It is not a count of the birds over your roof: it is the mean density across the whole air column, most of it between 200 and 3,000 metres up.
  • It does not tell species apart. A weather radar cannot know whether the echo is a thrush or a warbler.
  • In autumn and in the south, part of the signal may be insects. Separating them needs flight speed, and the European archive lacks it in France since 2021 and in about half of the bird layers of the renewed Spanish radars (see “Two gaps in the data”).
  • The forecast decays with lead time: the first night is reliable, the seventh much less so.
  • The four Spanish cities with a renewed radar are the model's hard case (it ranks their nights reasonably well, but the profile arrives truncated to six layers). Firm decisions there are better left until the 2027 season.
